## 1. The JPL "Levels of Edit" (Van Buren & Buehler)

**Status: PRIMARY.** I fetched the actual document, not a description of it.

**Citation:** Van Buren, Robert, and Mary Fran Buehler. *The Levels of Edit*, 2nd ed. JPL
Publication 80-1. Pasadena, CA: Jet Propulsion Laboratory, California Institute of
Technology, January 1980. (1st ed. published March 1976 as JPL SP 43-28.) Full text:
NASA Technical Reports Server, document ID 19800011701,
<https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19800011701/downloads/19800011701.pdf>. Fetched
2026 (this session). The PDF is an OCR'd scan; OCR is imperfect in places, but the
passages quoted below were checked against the scan and are legible and unambiguous.

### SOURCE

**What the system is.** Nine "types" of edit are combined cumulatively into five
"levels."

> "The editorial process is analyzed, and five levels of edit are identified. These
> levels represent cumulative combinations of nine types of edit: Coordination, Policy,
> Integrity, Screening, Copy Clarification, Format, Mechanical Style, Language, and
> Substantive." — Abstract

> "Each of the [nine] types of edit consists of a number of specific editorial functions
> ... Five cumulative combinations of the types of edit, as performed at JPL, have been
> identified as levels of edit (Table 1)." — Section II

**Table 1 ("Types and levels of edit"), reproduced from the source (X = included at that
level; levels run 1 = most thorough to 5 = least):**

| Type            | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 | Level 4 | Level 5 |
|-----------------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Coordination    | X | X | X | X | X |
| Policy          | X | X | X | X | X |
| Integrity       | X | X | X | X |   |
| Screening       | X | X | X | X |   |
| Copy Clarification | X | X | X |   |   |
| Format          | X | X | X |   |   |
| Mechanical Style| X | X |   |   |   |
| Language        | X | X |   |   |   |
| Substantive     | X |   |   |   |   |

**Why the levels are cumulative "packages" rather than a free choice of individual
types** — this is the JPL argument for standard, named levels instead of ad hoc
combinations:

> "The types of edit listed in Table 1 may be used independently, or in some combination
> of the types of edit that does not fit one of the edit levels; for example, an author
> may require an Integrity Edit and a Language Edit and nothing more. In such a case, the
> two types of edit would be performed without reference to the levels of edit. But this
> approach is valid only for those manuscripts that do not have an institutionally
> imposed minimum level of edit... There is, however, a definite advantage in combining
> the types of edit into various levels, since the levels relate to the kinds of products
> published, the quality of those products, and the cost." — Section II

**The argument for strict, named boundaries between types (what each level must NOT
touch), given in the Preface to the 2nd edition:**

> "If an editor is required to do a Format Edit, but not a Mechanical Style Edit (as
> often happens), the editor must know exactly what to do and what to leave undone. The
> Mechanical Style Edit lifts the effort into another level of edit (from Level 3 to
> Level 2) and could increase the cost. If the job is estimated on the basis of a Level 3
> edit, the editor may overrun the estimate — with no justification — if he or she does
> not know the precise limits of the effort required." — Preface to the Second Edition

**Important correction to a common assumption:** the JPL levels are **not** a sequential
pipeline that every document passes through one stage at a time (that is the modern
trade-publishing sense of "developmental → substantive → line → copy → proof," discussed
in §2 below). At JPL a *level* is chosen once, up front, per document/class, based on
budget, schedule, and the class of publication (JPL used publication "Class A/B/C" to
help decide this). The cumulative structure guarantees that a higher level always
includes everything a lower level includes, but a single manuscript does not
necessarily receive Level 5 first, then 4, then 3, etc.

> "In a Level 5 edit, the editor verifies that JPL policy has not been violated, [and]
> routes the manuscript... The Level 4 edit applies mainly to publications produced from
> camera-ready copy... The Level 2 edit is often used for such publications as journal
> articles and meeting papers, where a specific mechanical style is required by the
> publisher. And in a Level 1 edit, the full range of editorial capabilities is applied to
> produce a first-class publication." — Section II

**Definitions of the individual types most relevant to "generative vs. diagnostic"
rules:**

Format vs. Mechanical Style (the JPL document treats this boundary as easily confused
and worth a dedicated section, Section V):

> "Format concerns the visual aspect. It answers the question, 'What does it look like?'
> ... Format deals with the aesthetic or design aspects of a publication, and format
> decisions are based primarily on subjective, artistic judgments... Mechanical style, on
> the other hand, is related to the content of the publication. Stylistic decisions are
> based more on meaning than on form; they deal with symbols that represent meaning." —
> Section V

Mechanical Style Edit (Section VI.G) — consistency of already-chosen conventions, not
choice of the conventions themselves:

> "The Mechanical Style Edit is performed to bring the mechanics of the text and figures
> into consistent conformity with a specified style. Such mechanical aspects include, for
> example, capitalization, abbreviations, use of numbers, use of bold face and italics
> for symbols, and sequencing of reference, figure, and table citations." — Section VI.G

Language Edit (Section VI.H) — the passage most directly relevant to "diagnostic, not a
matter of the editor's taste":

> "The Language Edit is an in-depth review concerned with the way in which the ideas in a
> report are expressed, regardless of the format... or mechanical style... All editorial
> changes in a Language Edit are made on the basis of specific and identifiable reasons
> rather than the personal preferences of the editor." — Section VI.H

> "We think that an editor should never ignore the spirit of the language, should never
> sacrifice the essence of the communication to a convenient application of prefabricated
> rules, methods, procedures, or conditions. A Language Edit requires a sense of balance,
> of appropriateness. A heavy-handed editor, for instance, in an attempt to eliminate
> wordiness, may completely excise the rhetorical effectiveness of an author's prose; a
> timid editor, by allowing inappropriate or unbridled rhetoric, may allow the language to
> obscure the meaning." — Section VI.H

Substantive Edit (Section VI.I) — content/meaning, explicitly the top (most expensive,
least standardized) type:

> "The Substantive Edit deals with the meaningful content of the publication. The edit
> includes, but is not limited to, coherence of the individual parts; for example, the
> scope of the publication should be accurately reflected in the Title, the Abstract, and
> the introductory section, and the information in all three areas should be consistent."
> — Section VI.I

Integrity Edit (Section VI.C) — an explicit example in the source of a type stating what
it must NOT do, and where the boundary work belongs instead:

> "An Integrity Edit is concerned primarily with ensuring that the parts of a publication
> match. For example, if 'Figure 1' is cited, an Integrity Edit will determine whether
> Figure 1 is included in the report. However, an Integrity Edit will not resolve any
> apparent inconsistencies or contradictions in the meaning expressed in different parts
> of the report. Such discrepancies are discussed in Substantive Edit." — Section VI.C

A second, explicit note distinguishing *language* parallelism (order/sequencing of
words/symbols — a Language Edit matter) from *substantive* parallelism (order/weighting
of ideas — a Substantive Edit matter), which is close kin to the "generative vs.
diagnostic" split the user is investigating:

> "There is also a fundamental difference between parallelism of language... and the
> concepts of parallelism and subordination discussed under Substantive Edit. Parallelism
> of language is concerned primarily with the order and sequencing of symbols, words, and
> phrases, as distinct from the underlying ideas to which they relate... the parallelism
> and subordination discussed under Substantive Edit refer to ideas and concepts, as
> distinct from the words and phrases used to express them... the distinction between
> language parallelism and substantive parallelism should be kept clearly in mind when
> considering the edit level involved." — Section VI.H

### MY CONCLUSIONS (evidential status: THEORETICAL — this is a named formalism, not a
study)

- The JPL system is a **classification/costing formalism**, not a set of writing rules.
  Its unit of analysis is "type of editorial function," and its purpose is to let a
  manager and an editor agree, in advance, on the *precise boundary of what will and
  will not be touched* — this is stated explicitly as the reason distinctions are drawn
  "rigorously" even where they feel arbitrary (Section V, on Format vs. Style).
- The system gives **no generative writing advice at all** ("lead with the decision" has
  no home here). It is entirely diagnostic/procedural: for each of the nine types, it
  lists what the editor checks and fixes, and for several types (Integrity, Language) it
  explicitly says what the type must leave alone and hands off to a different type.
- The "do them in order" intuition in the user's question does not map cleanly onto this
  source: JPL's cumulative levels are a **menu of pre-committed depths**, not a
  **required sequence through the same manuscript**. The order-of-passes argument (structure
  before sentences before mechanics) is a *different* claim, made more directly by
  Einsohn and by the general trade-publishing pipeline (see §2), not by the JPL system
  itself.

## 2. Lineage after JPL: Los Alamos, Rude, and others (as reported by Wikipedia)

**Status: SECONDARY.** I read Wikipedia's "Levels of edit" article, not the underlying
Los Alamos or STC-conference sources it cites. I did not independently verify these
downstream claims against their primary documents (see Gaps, §8).

**Citation:** "Levels of edit," Wikipedia, The Free Encyclopedia, revision of 9 July
2026, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels_of_edit>. Fetched 2026 (this session).

### SOURCE

> "In 1978, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)... found a need to clearly 'define the
> endeavor of technical editing'... Robert Van Buren and Mary Fran Buehler, of the JPL,
> developed five levels of edit defined by nine editorial categories... JPL published a
> second edition in 1980, which was distributed to the public by the United States
> Government Printing Office (GPO). After the GPO's supply of the second edition was
> depleted, the Society for Technical Communication (STC) made an exact reprint of the
> second edition available."

> "Drawing on Van Buren and Buehler's work, in 1985 the writing and editing group at the
> Los Alamos National Laboratory established four levels of edit for technical reports. A
> team at the laboratory revised the work following a 1994 survey that indicated the
> original four levels no longer met authors' needs. The Los Alamos revision presented
> three 'author-based' levels of edit (proofreading edit, grammar edit, and full edit)
> whose goals were to 'simplify the editing process, focus editing on improving technical
> clarity, and ensure that value was added in editing.'" — citing Prono, J., DeLanoy, M.,
> Deupree, R., Skiby, J., and B. Thompson, "Developing New Levels of Edit," Los Alamos
> National Laboratory, 1998.

> "For students learning technical communication, Carolyn D. Rude reduced the levels of
> edit to just two types: 1) comprehensive editing and 2) copy editing. Comprehensive
> editing encompasses 'the full range of document qualities, including content,
> organization, and design as well as grammar and punctuation, with the goal of making a
> document more usable, suitable for its purpose and readers, and comprehension,' while
> copy editing involves editing grammar, formatting, and sentence structure." — citing
> Rude, C. D., *Technical Editing*, 4th ed. (New York: Pearson Longman, 2006).

> "As web-based on-demand proofreading and editing services have become increasingly
> available, the number of edit levels and editing schemes has increased in variety. For
> example, three cumulative levels is a common scheme, typically 1) light, 2) medium, and
> 3) heavy editing."

### MY CONCLUSIONS (evidential status: THEORETICAL/CRAFT mix, per the underlying claim —
the Los Alamos and Rude schemes are proposed formalisms with stated design goals, not
tested results)

- The trend across all of these variants (Los Alamos, Rude, the "light/medium/heavy"
  vendor scheme) is toward *fewer, coarser levels* than JPL's nine-types/five-levels
  system, and toward levels that map to a **document's condition and purpose** rather
  than to a cost-estimation grid for an in-house lab. This confirms the JPL system is
  the most granular/formal named version, and later versions traded precision for
  usability — worth knowing if the goal is to build a *minimal* practical system rather
  than reproduce JPL's full apparatus.

### SOURCE (Wikipedia's paraphrase/quotation, attributed by it to Einsohn and to CMOS)

> "*The Chicago Manual of Style* states that manuscript editing encompasses 'simple
> mechanical corrections (mechanical editing) through sentence-level interventions
> (linear editing) to substantial remedial work on literary style and clarity,
> disorganized passages, baggy prose, muddled tables and figures, and the like
> (substantive editing).'" — cited to Einsohn (2011), pp. 1–5 [note: Wikipedia attributes
> the quoted words to CMOS but footnotes the page range to Einsohn; I cannot tell from the
> secondary source alone whether this is Einsohn quoting CMOS, or a citation error in the
> Wikipedia article. Flagged as unverified — see Gaps, §8.]

> "Copy editing has three levels: light, medium, and heavy. Depending on the budget and
> scheduling of the publication, the publisher will let the copy editor know what level
> of editing to employ." — cited to Einsohn (2011), p. 12

> "Although copy editors are generally expected to make simple revisions to smooth
> awkward passages, they do not have a license to rewrite a text line by line, nor do they
> prepare material on an author's behalf... copy editors are expected to query structural
> and organizational problems, but they are not expected to fix these problems. In
> addition, copy editors do not normally engage in 'developmental editing'..."

> "The copy editor strives to improve clarity, coherence, consistency and correctness —
> otherwise known as the '4 Cs', each of which serves the copy editor's 'cardinal C',
> which is communication." — cited to Einsohn (2011), p. 3

**Wikipedia's stated boundary between copy editing and line editing** (its own
synthesis, not directly cited to a single page of Einsohn in this passage):

> "Copy editing: This form of editing looks at the text from a broader perspective,
> ensuring consistency in style, tone, and voice. It also checks for factual accuracy and
> potential legal issues. Line editing: Line editing is a more in-depth review of each
> line in the manuscript. It focuses on the creative content, writing style and language
> use at the sentence and paragraph level."

**Querying**, again attributed to Einsohn (2011), pp. 7–10:

> "Copy editors often need to query their authors to address questions, comments, or
> explanations... The copy editor must consider when to query and the length and tone of
> their queries, as querying too often or seldom, cryptically, or sarcastically can result
> in a negative relationship between the copy editor and the author."

### MY CONCLUSIONS (evidential status: CRAFT — this is professional practice literature,
not an empirical study)

- Even at second hand, Einsohn's book is doing the same kind of work as JPL: it defines
  copyediting by **exclusion** ("do not have a license to rewrite line by line," "do not
  normally engage in developmental editing") as much as by inclusion. The recurring move
  across every source in this cluster is: *name the type of intervention, then say what
  it explicitly may not do, and hand that off by name to a different, more senior type of
  edit.*
- The "4 Cs / cardinal C" formulation (clarity, coherence, consistency, correctness →
  communication) is the closest thing in this literature to a compact definition of what
  copyediting is *for*, as opposed to what it *checks*. It is presented as a professional
  maxim, not derived from a study — I am marking it CRAFT, not EMPIRICAL, and Wikipedia's
  citation gives no indication Einsohn frames it otherwise.
- I could not confirm Wikipedia's CMOS quotation independently against CMOS's own text
  (paywalled — see §6) or against Einsohn's own pages (inaccessible). Treat that specific
  quoted sentence as **unverified pending access to either primary source**.

## 4. The style sheet — what it is and how it is used

**Status: mixed.** The definitional material (CIEP glossary, CIEP/SfEP fact sheet) is
PRIMARY — I read the professional body's own published pages. The practitioner
walkthrough (An American Editor blog) is PRIMARY as a first-person account of one
editor's practice, but its evidential status is CRAFT (one practitioner's method, not a
studied norm). Chicago Manual of Style's own definition is **not accessible** (paywalled
full text); I can only confirm the *existence and location* of the relevant section from
its public table of contents (PRIMARY, but TOC-only).

### SOURCE — CIEP editorial glossary (professional body, UK)

**Citation:** Chartered Institute of Editing and Proofreading (CIEP), "Editorial
Glossary," <https://www.ciep.uk/resource/editorial-glossary.html>. Fetched 2026 (this
session). No publication date given on page; CIEP is the 2019 rebrand of the Society for
Editors and Proofreaders (SfEP).

> "**style sheet:** a detailed record of the variable spellings, hyphenation,
> capitalisation, etc used in a particular document, which records in detail the
> editorial style applied in the text. Copyeditors should routinely compile a style sheet
> when working on a text and this may be passed to the proofreader so they can see what
> style was intended to be applied consistently."

> "**copyediting:** editing the copy (content) to match the client's brief, the needs of
> the typesetter or designer and the expectations of the target audience. The copyeditor
> follows a house style or custom style sheet to make the content (text, references,
> illustrations etc) clear and consistent throughout, correcting errors in grammar,
> spelling, vocabulary and punctuation while using templates, codes or formatting to make
> the document ready for typesetting or uploading."

### SOURCE — SfEP/CIEP fact sheet distinguishing copyediting from proofreading

**Citation:** Society for Editors and Proofreaders [now CIEP], "Proofreading vs
Copy-editing," factsheet, written by Margaret Hunter (information director), © 2018.
Fetched as PDF via mirror
<https://irp-cdn.multiscreensite.com/66b146fc/files/uploaded/SfEP-factsheet-editing-proofreading.pdf>
2026 (this session); the same document is linked from CIEP's current knowledge hub at
<https://www.ciep.uk/resource/fact-sheets-free-pce.html>.

> "Copy-editing is... applying your house style or ensuring consistency in spellings,
> capital letters, date format, numbers, units … [vs.] preparing a comprehensive style
> guide for you or your organisation [which is explicitly NOT copy-editing]."

> "Copy-editing is... querying obvious factual inaccuracy [vs.] research or
> fact-checking [which is explicitly NOT copy-editing]."

> "Proofreading is... all page elements consistent, placed correctly and style decisions
> applied [vs.] reordering or changing structure or layout [NOT proofreading]."

### SOURCE — practitioner walkthrough of building a style sheet (fiction editing)

**Status: PRIMARY (a working editor's own account), evidential status CRAFT.**

**Citation:** Schneider, Amy J. "Thinking Fiction: The Style Sheets — Part I: General
Style." *An American Editor* (blog, ed. Rich Adin), 19 January 2015.
<https://americaneditor.wordpress.com/2015/01/19/thinking-fiction-the-style-sheets-part-i-general-style/>.
Fetched 2026 (this session). (Schneider later authored the professionally published

> "Of course, as for a nonfiction copyedit, you will compile a style sheet. If a style
> sheet is available from a previous book by that author or a previous book in the
> series, use that as your starting point... I actually maintain four style sheets
> (general style, characters, places, and timeline) for each fiction edit for ease of

> Confirmed section list (numbers and titles exactly as published): "2.48 Levels of

### MY CONCLUSIONS (evidential status: THEORETICAL for the guide/sheet distinction as a
structural claim; CRAFT for the "how it's built" account)

- **This is the cleanest confirmation in the whole cluster of the guide/sheet
  distinction the user asked about.** CMOS's own outline keeps "levels of manuscript
  editing" (§§2.48–2.53, definitional/procedural — what kind of edit this is) structurally
  separate from "keeping an editorial style sheet" (§2.55, a single numbered section) and
  separate again from "writing author comments and queries" (§2.69) and "inserting
  comments and queries" (§2.87, in the electronic-markup part of the chapter). The manual
  itself treats **the rule system (style guide), the per-document record (style sheet),
  and the query mechanism** as three distinct topics with three distinct places in its own
  table of contents — not because I inferred it, but because that is literally how the
  chapter is organized.
- The CIEP/SfEP material converges independently on the same shape: a **style guide is a
  standing document an organization owns** ("preparing a comprehensive style guide for
  you or your organisation" is called out as a *separate, non-copyediting* task); a
  **style sheet is a per-document artifact a copyeditor produces while doing the edit**,
  and its job is specifically to record *choices between two acceptable options*
  ("variable spellings, hyphenation, capitalisation... used in a particular document").
  This exactly matches the user's own framing: the style sheet is the enforcement record
  for a decision the guide could only leave open.
- The style sheet's second stated function — handed to the proofreader "so they can see
  what style was intended to be applied consistently" (CIEP glossary) — shows it is not
  just a private aid; it is a **transfer artifact between editorial stages**, which is a
  mechanism a generative writing rule cannot provide (a writing rule has no persistent
  memory of what was decided last time).

## 5. Carol Fisher Saller, *The Subversive Copy Editor*, and editorial restraint

**Status: mixed.** I could not obtain the book itself (Internet Archive's copy of the
2nd edition is an empty/metadata-only "opensource" upload with no text files — checked
and confirmed; other Archive.org copies are lending-restricted). What I have instead are
(a) a direct excerpt from the book's own introduction, reproduced verbatim by the
publisher on the CMOS Shop Talk blog to promote the 2nd edition — I count this as
**PRIMARY** (it is the author's own words, unmodified, just relocated), and (b) essays
Saller wrote herself for CMOS Shop Talk under her "Editor's Corner" byline, which are
**PRIMARY** first-person statements of her editorial views, though they are not the
book.

**Citation (blog post, with verbatim book excerpt):** "Two new books from the editors at
*The Chicago Manual of Style*," *CMOS Shop Talk* (University of Chicago Press), 18
April 2016. <https://cmosshoptalk.com/2016/04/18/two-new-books-from-the-editors-at-the-chicago-manual-of-style/>.
Fetched 2026 (this session).

### SOURCE

The book's own introduction, quoted directly on the publisher's blog:

> "For students, professors, copy editors, businesspeople, and writers who are sometimes
> dogged by indecision or confusion over rules of style and grammar; for those of you who
> know the rules but agonize over when or whether to apply them; for those who copyedit
> for a living and those who don't and those who would like to. In the following pages, I
> hope to soothe and encourage and lend power. I am not going to do this, however, by
> setting your teacher/student/author/colleague/boss/editor straight. And I'm not going to
> help with your homework. You won't learn the fundamentals of copyediting from me.
> Rather, consider this a 'relationship' book, because I'm going to talk about the main
> relationships in your work life — with the writer, with your colleagues, and with
> yourself — in ways that you might not have considered before. Ways that might be called
> subversive."

The same blog post's own framing of the book (publisher copy, not a direct Saller
quote, but published alongside and endorsed by the author's own blog presence):

> "She has negotiated many a standoff between author and editor. Her guide for keeping
> the peace advocates seeing authors as partners instead of adversaries. She also teaches
> copy editors to embrace the sometimes heretical idea that 'it's not a matter of being
> correct or incorrect. It's only a style.'"

**Citation (Saller's own essay on over-correction and restraint):** Saller, Carol.
"Who's a 'Lazy' Copyeditor?" *CMOS Shop Talk*, Editor's Corner, 23 May 2017.
<https://cmosshoptalk.com/2017/05/23/whos-a-lazy-copyeditor/>. Fetched 2026 (this
session). Byline confirmed at foot of post: "Carol Saller's books include *The
Subversive Copy Editor*..."

> "Sticklers stickle because they don't know better and aren't interested in listening
> or learning more. They seem to think that calling people names is a credible debate
> tactic... Professional writers and editors stay informed and up to date and appreciate
> language in all its changing, wayward glory. One thing they're not: lazy."

### MY CONCLUSIONS (evidential status: CRAFT throughout — Saller's material is
professional opinion/experience, explicitly framed by her as a "relationship book," not
a study)

- Saller's stated position, even at this remove, is *not* a set of new diagnostic rules.
  It is a **meta-level claim about the editor's authority**: many things a copyeditor is
  tempted to change are not errors but *style choices* ("it's not a matter of being
  correct or incorrect. It's only a style") — and treating a style choice as an error is
  the specific failure mode she is naming. This is a direct, named articulation of
  over-editing as illegitimate use of editorial power, which is exactly the "restraint"

## 6. "Query, don't change" — the querying convention

**Status: PRIMARY for the professional-body and publisher material below.** I looked
specifically for the exact phrase "query, don't change" (and near variants: "when in
doubt, query," "query, do not change," "if in doubt, query," "stet and query") using
exact-phrase web search. **None of these exact phrases returned any indexed hits** —
this is a genuine gap, not a formatting artifact (I re-ran the searches after confirming
the search backend was returning results for other, unrelated queries in the same
session). I am treating "query, don't change" as **the user's own compact label for a
convention that is well documented under other, longer phrasings** — not as a
verified fixed term of art in the literature. This should be corrected if a source
naming it exactly is found later.

### SOURCE — CIEP, on what a query is and what makes a good one

**Citation:** Tomlinson, Philippa. "What are queries, and how should an author
respond?" Chartered Institute of Editing and Proofreading, *Knowledge Hub*, 19 January
2024. <https://www.ciep.uk/resource/queries-author.html>. Fetched 2026 (this session).

> "The copyeditor will be the first person to go through the manuscript in really close
> detail. They will be interrogating the text with a critical eye: not in the negative
> sense of finding fault or pointing out mistakes, but with the aim of making the text
> the best possible version of itself. And this involves asking questions of the author
> — AQs or author queries — on anything the copyeditor can't resolve themselves or on any
> changes they have made which need the author's approval or confirmation."

> "The copyeditor will also decide what not to query and will use their skills and
> expertise to make small-scale, non-contentious changes or corrections to spelling,
> grammar, punctuation, facts such as dates and so on themselves — guided by the five
> 'c's of copyediting to make the text as clear, consistent, correct, concise and
> comprehensive as possible."

Concrete example queries given by CIEP (worth keeping as the operational definition of
"good query" — specific, falsifiable, and tied to a located point in the text):

> "'Mouth movements' and 'Handwriting' are now subheadings under 'Movement'. Are you
> happy with this?"
> "Robertson 2019 isn't included in the references, but there is a Robertson 2018
> listed. Please check dates/details and let me know any changes required."
> "You use 'mute' here — do you mean 'moot'?"

This source explicitly states copyediting is a **hybrid** of silent, small-scale fixing
plus queries for anything bigger or ambiguous — not a pure "always query, never touch"
regime.

### SOURCE — a publisher's own practice (hybrid model, edit-then-confirm)

**Citation:** Lenkei, Alex. "An author's guide to copyediting queries." Cell Press
CrossTalk Blog, 4 February 2020.
<https://crosstalk.cell.com/blog/an-authors-guide-to-copyediting-queries>. Fetched 2026
(this session).

> "These questions, which we call author queries or copyediting queries, are designed to
> clear up any syntactical confusion and ensure clarity and consistency across a
> manuscript... We might also ask you to confirm whether edits we made to a sentence
> change the intended scientific meaning and, if they do, to correct it." — quoting Tracy
> Campbell, production editor of *Cell* and *Patterns*: "Queries are an essential part of
> scientific publishing because they allow us to enhance a paper's clarity and
> readability."

### SOURCE — an in-house institutional guide stating the restraint principle plainly

**Citation:** University of Edinburgh, Communications and Marketing, "Guide to
copy-editing" (PDF, undated, no version number given).
<https://edwebcontent.ed.ac.uk/sites/default/files/polopoly/fileManager/Guide-to-copy-editing.pdf>.
Fetched 2026 (this session).

> "Try to keep the original writer's voice as much as possible. Avoid changing the
> meaning of a sentence, statement or phrase unless you are confident the original is
> erroneous. If you think the piece needs a complete rewrite or it has gone way over the
> agreed word count, ask your manager whether it should be sent back to the writer with
> clear instructions, or whether you are permitted to rewrite/cut it yourself."

### MY CONCLUSIONS (evidential status: CRAFT — this is universally received professional
practice, not something any of these sources present as tested)

- Across three independent institutional sources (a UK professional body, a scientific
  publisher, and a university communications office), the actual practice is **not** a
  strict "never touch it, always ask" rule. It is a **threshold rule**: fix small,
  low-risk, unambiguous things silently (spelling, punctuation, obvious grammar); query
  anything that (a) might change meaning, (b) requires information the editor doesn't
  have, or (c) the editor "genuinely doesn't understand." The CIEP source states this
  threshold explicitly via its "five Cs."
- What makes a query "good," per the CIEP's own examples, is not politeness but
  **specificity and locatability**: each example query names the exact word, exact
  location, and the exact fork in the decision ("do you mean X or Y"), rather than a
  vague comment like "unclear." That specificity is the actual transferable device here —
  more useful than the slogan "query, don't change," which I could not source as a named
  term.
- I recommend the writing standard use the CIEP's operational framing (silent fix below
  a stated threshold of risk; specific, located query above it) rather than a slogan,
  since the slogan itself is unverified as a term of art.

## 7. Chicago Manual of Style — guide vs. sheet, general

Already covered in §4 with full sourcing (TOC-only access). Repeating the single
sharpest point here because it answers the user's direct question:

### MY CONCLUSIONS (evidential status: THEORETICAL — structural/definitional, drawn from
the manual's own organization, not a study)

- CMOS 17's chapter 2 keeps three things separate *by section number*, not just by my
  inference: **§§2.48–2.52 "Principles of Manuscript Editing"** (what kind of edit this
  is, and how much discretion the editor has — this is the *guide*, i.e., standing rules
  that apply across all documents); **§2.55 "Keeping an editorial style sheet"** (the
  per-document record); and **§2.69 / §2.87 "queries"** (the mechanism for handling a
  case the guide and the sheet don't settle). A style *guide* is authored once, applies
  to every document, and answers questions in general. A style *sheet* is authored fresh
  for each document (or inherited from a prior volume, per the Schneider excerpt in §4),
  and answers only the questions this particular document actually raised. A *query* is
  what happens when neither the guide nor the sheet can settle the question without the
  author.

## 8. Direct answer to "how does an editing guide differ in kind from a writing guide"

**This section is entirely MY CONCLUSIONS, synthesized across §§1–7. It is not itself
sourced to a single passage — I am stating this plainly per the no-blending rule.**
Evidential status: THEORETICAL synthesis, built from THEORETICAL and CRAFT sources
above; I found no EMPIRICAL (studied, measured) source anywhere in this cluster. That
absence is itself worth reporting: none of JPL, CMOS, Einsohn (as reported), Saller, or
CIEP frame their claims as the result of a controlled study. They are all professional
formalisms or professional craft advice.

The literature in this cluster does **not** claim editing and writing guides are the
same kind of thing wearing different clothes. It draws the line in a specific, recurring
way across every independent source consulted (JPL, CMOS, CIEP/SfEP, and — at second
hand — Einsohn):

1. **What it contains.** A writing rule ("lead with the decision") is *generative*: it
   tells you what to produce, and it is stated once, in the imperative, for every future
   document. An editing type/level (JPL's nine types; CMOS's mechanical/substantive
   split; Einsohn's "4 Cs") is *diagnostic*: it names a category of defect to find in an
   already-existing text, and — critically — every source that defines an editing type
   also states what that type must **not** touch and where that other work belongs
   (JPL's Integrity Edit explicitly disclaims resolving meaning conflicts and hands them
   to Substantive Edit; CMOS separates mechanical editing (§2.49) from substantive
   editing (§2.50); Saller frames much of what looks like error as "only a style," i.e.,
   not this type's business at all).
2. **What it is attached to.** A writing guide is stated *context-free*: the rule is
   supposed to hold for any document of the given kind. An editing apparatus produces
   two further, document-bound artifacts that a writing guide structurally cannot: the
   **style sheet** (a record of *which* of two permitted options this document chose —
   CIEP glossary, CMOS §2.55) and the **query** (a flagged, unresolved case handed back to
   the author — CMOS §2.69/§2.87, CIEP's worked examples, Cell Press's practice). Neither
   artifact is a rule; both are decisions or open questions specific to one manuscript.
3. **At what moment it is used.** Every source that discusses order (JPL's classes and
   levels, CMOS's chapter sequence, the SfEP factsheet's "raw or draft text" vs. "text in
   final layout") places editing work **after** a draft exists and treats it as bounded
   by what has already been decided — an editor is diagnosing conformance to decisions
   (the document's own style sheet, the org's style guide, the author's intent) rather
   than generating first drafts of content or sentences. This is the JPL Language Edit's
   explicit rule: changes must be made "on the basis of specific and identifiable
   reasons rather than the personal preferences of the editor" — i.e., the editor is not
   licensed to write according to their own generative taste; they are licensed to check
   against a named, externally fixed standard (a dictionary, a style manual, the
   document's own style sheet, or an author's stated intent recovered via query).
4. **Who owns the decision.** A writing guide's author is deciding for the writer, in
   advance. An editing apparatus is explicit, in every source here, that many
   corrigible-looking things are not errors at all but choices the *author* or a
   *prior, document-specific decision* owns — and the edit's job is to enforce
   consistency with that decision (via the style sheet) or to surface the choice
   explicitly (via a query) rather than to silently resolve it according to the editor's
   own preference. This is the throughline from JPL ("specific and identifiable
   reasons... rather than personal preferences") through CIEP ("query... anything the
   copyeditor can't resolve themselves") to Saller ("it's not a matter of being correct
   or incorrect. It's only a style").

**Bottom line for the writing standard:** the user's suspicion is well supported by this
literature, and stated more precisely than "generative vs. diagnostic" alone: a
generative rule binds the writer, once, before any draft exists, and is the same for
every document. A diagnostic rule binds a *reviewer of an existing draft*, and every
version of it in this literature comes paired with (a) an explicit statement of what it
must NOT fix, and (b) a mechanism — a style sheet or a query — for handling document-
specific decisions that the rule itself cannot make. A single "writing standard"
document can state generative rules validly, but it cannot, by itself, do the job of a
style sheet (record which of two options THIS document chose) or a query log (record
what was asked and left open) — those need to be separate, per-document artifacts, not
more rules in the same document.
